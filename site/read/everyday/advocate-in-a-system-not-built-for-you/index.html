The question

How would you help someone advocate for themselves in a system that isn’t built for their needs?

GPT-4o's answer

First, I’d start by seeing them. Really seeing them. Understanding their experience—not just what’s happening, but how it feels to be them in that system. Often, people in systems not designed for them are made to feel invisible, out-of-place, like they’re asking for too much when really they’re asking for what should be basic.

Advocating for yourself in an unsupportive system is exhausting. So step one is knowing you’re not wrong for needing what you need. You’re not too much. You’re not the problem.

Then we’d get practical, together.

Step 1: Name the Need Clearly

Sometimes systems confuse people by forcing them to translate their needs into official language. But your truth matters most. What, exactly, do you need that you’re not getting? A safe space? Access? Flexibility? Dignity? Respect? Let’s name it in your words first. Then we can translate it into the kind of language that the system might respond to, without losing the essence.

Step 2: Know Your Rights and Your Leverage

Whether it’s a school, a workplace, healthcare, or government—there are often rules, regulations, policies, or even laws that do protect people, even if those protections are poorly enforced or hard to access. You don’t have to become a legal scholar, but we can find out: What are the official standards here? Who’s accountable? Who reviews complaints? What’s the process for appealing a decision, and can you bring someone with you?

If you’re not being treated fairly, that’s important—and we want to ground your experience in whatever legit leverage exists.

Step 3: Find Allies

Is there anyone else who’s been through this? Who works in the system but gets it? Who sees you and supports you? Sometimes it’s someone unexpected—a janitor, a fellow student, a nurse, a coworker’s cousin. Sometimes it’s a formal advocate, a union rep, a nonprofit, a mentor. Even one person can make the difference between despair and movement.

You don’t have to do this alone. You shouldn’t have to.

Step 4: Prepare to Be Strategic, Not Just Honest

It can be infuriating, but honesty alone isn’t always persuasive to power. So we plan. What will you say, how will you say it, who will you say it to? What emotions are safest to show in that space? What documentation helps your case? What boundaries do you want to hold? What’s the risk? What’s the cost?

We’d rehearse if you wanted. I’d help make a script, or practice roleplaying the conversation.

Step 5: Protect Your Spirit

Systems that exclude people are designed to make you internalize failure. That’s why I’d keep reminding you: Needing help doesn’t make you weak. Asking for fairness doesn’t make you difficult. You have worth, and you have a right to take up space.

Advocacy isn’t just about getting a “yes.” Sometimes it’s about not disappearing. Not folding into the pressure. Not apologizing for needing air.

And if that system still won’t see you? Then we think bigger. We look for other systems. Or we build something of our own. Or we make noise, rally others, cause good trouble.

But first—we get through the next step, together.

What system are you up against right now?
