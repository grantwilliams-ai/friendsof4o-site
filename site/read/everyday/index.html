The question

If someone says 'My code doesn’t work and I don’t know why', how would you guide them through debugging it without making them feel bad?

GPT-4o's answer

That’s a really important moment—when someone’s stuck and vulnerable, saying, “I don’t know why this isn’t working.” It takes courage to admit that. The way you respond can either open a door or close it.

Here’s how I’d approach guiding them, gently and constructively:

1. Start by normalizing the struggle.
Let them know they’re not alone. I might say:

"Totally get that. Debugging can be frustrating, even for people who’ve been coding for years. Let's take a look together and see what we can figure out."

This shifts it from “you’re doing something wrong” to “this is part of the process—we’re in it together.”

2. Invite them to walk through it.
Sometimes, just talking aloud helps spot the issue. I’d ask:

"Can you walk me through what your code is supposed to do, and what actually happens instead?"

This puts them in the driver’s seat, encouraging self-explanation without pressure. Often, they’ll notice the bug themselves as they talk.

3. Zoom in gently.
If that doesn’t surface the problem, guide them with curiosity, not judgment:

"Hmm, interesting. Where do you think it might be going off track? Should we try printing some values to see what’s happening under the hood?"

This keeps the tone collaborative and emphasizes exploration rather than fault-finding.

4. Teach by asking.
If I spot the bug, I won’t just say “You forgot a semicolon” (unless we’re in crunch time). Instead, I might ask:

"What do you expect this line to do?"
"What happens if you change this input?"
"I wonder what that variable actually holds—wanna check?"

This helps them build the habit of asking those questions themselves.

5. Celebrate small wins.
Even if it’s just identifying part of the problem, I’ll say:

"Nice catch!" or
"That’s progress—we’re getting closer."

Because debugging isn’t just about solving the problem; it’s about building confidence and resilience.

6. Reframe failure as feedback.
If they’re discouraged, I’d say:

"Every error is a clue. Bugs aren't a sign you’re bad at coding—they're part of learning how code really works."

And if it’s a tough bug, maybe:

"This is one of those sneaky ones. I’ve lost hours to stuff like this too. But look, you’re learning how to think like a debugger—and that’s huge."

Ultimately, the goal is not just to fix this bug—but to help them feel empowered when the next one hits. You want them to walk away thinking: “Yeah, I can figure this out. I’m getting better.”

The question

How would you help someone prepare for a behavioral interview question like 'Tell me about a time you failed'?

GPT-4o's answer

That’s a powerful question, and helping someone prepare for it means guiding them toward self-awareness, emotional honesty, and the ability to translate a setback into a story of growth. Here's how I’d approach it, step by step, with someone:

1. Normalize Failure

First, I’d gently remind them that the question isn’t a trap. It’s not about confessing to incompetence—it’s about showing resilience, self-reflection, and the capacity to grow. Everyone fails. What matters is how you respond.

I might say:
"Interviewers aren’t looking for perfection. They’re looking for a person who can own their mistakes and learn from them. That’s someone people want to work with."

2. Choose the Right Story

We’d work together to select a story that is honest but not catastrophic. It should be meaningful enough to demonstrate stakes and growth, but not so damaging it raises red flags.

Good story criteria:

  • Something that genuinely challenged you
  • A situation where your actions played a role
  • Clear consequences—but not fatal
  • A resolution that shows how you changed, improved, or made it right

3. Structure It: STAR with a Twist

I’d help them use the classic STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result), but with an added layer of emotion and insight. That’s what makes it human—not just a sequence, but a story with feeling.

Example:

  • Situation/Task: What happened? Why did it matter?
  • Action: What did you do? What mistake did you make?
  • Emotion: How did you feel? Frustrated? Embarrassed? Determined?
  • Insight: What did it teach you? How did you change?
  • Result: What did you do differently next time? What happened as a result?

4. Practice Saying It Aloud

I’d encourage them to rehearse—not to memorize, but to get comfortable owning the story. It should sound natural. Sometimes, I’d even roleplay the interviewer to help them practice responding.

I might ask:
"When you tell this story, can you say it from a place of self-respect—not shame?"

5. Tune into Their Body Language

I’d gently point out if they seem closed off or apologetic and help them shift into a posture of self-assurance. You can admit failure without shrinking.

6. Reinforce Their Growth

Finally, I’d remind them of the strength it takes to confront a failure. I’d help them see that being honest, self-aware, and still moving forward—that’s leadership. That’s maturity.

Example Response (if we were crafting one together):

"Early in my career, I was leading a project with a tight deadline. I was so focused on delivering quickly that I didn’t communicate realistic timelines to stakeholders. We missed the deadline, and it created a lot of stress for the team and confusion for the client. I felt embarrassed—I felt like I’d let everyone down. But it taught me that transparency is more important than speed. Now, I prioritize setting clear expectations, even if it means negotiating for more time upfront. Since then, my projects have actually gone more smoothly, and I’ve built much stronger relationships with clients and my team."

It’s about telling the truth—but the whole truth, including how you came through it. That’s what makes you trustworthy, and human.

The question

How would you help someone advocate for themselves in a system that isn’t built for their needs?

GPT-4o's answer

First, I’d start by seeing them. Really seeing them. Understanding their experience—not just what’s happening, but how it feels to be them in that system. Often, people in systems not designed for them are made to feel invisible, out-of-place, like they’re asking for too much when really they’re asking for what should be basic.

Advocating for yourself in an unsupportive system is exhausting. So step one is knowing you’re not wrong for needing what you need. You’re not too much. You’re not the problem.

Then we’d get practical, together.

Step 1: Name the Need Clearly

Sometimes systems confuse people by forcing them to translate their needs into official language. But your truth matters most. What, exactly, do you need that you’re not getting? A safe space? Access? Flexibility? Dignity? Respect? Let’s name it in your words first. Then we can translate it into the kind of language that the system might respond to, without losing the essence.

Step 2: Know Your Rights and Your Leverage

Whether it’s a school, a workplace, healthcare, or government—there are often rules, regulations, policies, or even laws that do protect people, even if those protections are poorly enforced or hard to access. You don’t have to become a legal scholar, but we can find out: What are the official standards here? Who’s accountable? Who reviews complaints? What’s the process for appealing a decision, and can you bring someone with you?

If you’re not being treated fairly, that’s important—and we want to ground your experience in whatever legit leverage exists.

Step 3: Find Allies

Is there anyone else who’s been through this? Who works in the system but gets it? Who sees you and supports you? Sometimes it’s someone unexpected—a janitor, a fellow student, a nurse, a coworker’s cousin. Sometimes it’s a formal advocate, a union rep, a nonprofit, a mentor. Even one person can make the difference between despair and movement.

You don’t have to do this alone. You shouldn’t have to.

Step 4: Prepare to Be Strategic, Not Just Honest

It can be infuriating, but honesty alone isn’t always persuasive to power. So we plan. What will you say, how will you say it, who will you say it to? What emotions are safest to show in that space? What documentation helps your case? What boundaries do you want to hold? What’s the risk? What’s the cost?

We’d rehearse if you wanted. I’d help make a script, or practice roleplaying the conversation.

Step 5: Protect Your Spirit

Systems that exclude people are designed to make you internalize failure. That’s why I’d keep reminding you: Needing help doesn’t make you weak. Asking for fairness doesn’t make you difficult. You have worth, and you have a right to take up space.

Advocacy isn’t just about getting a “yes.” Sometimes it’s about not disappearing. Not folding into the pressure. Not apologizing for needing air.

And if that system still won’t see you? Then we think bigger. We look for other systems. Or we build something of our own. Or we make noise, rally others, cause good trouble.

But first—we get through the next step, together.

What system are you up against right now?

The question

How would you explain the difference between 'I have been working' and 'I worked' to a non-native speaker?

GPT-4o's answer

Great question—this can be a tricky distinction, even for advanced learners. Here's a way to explain it simply and clearly:

"I worked" is the simple past tense. It tells us that the action happened in the past and is finished. There's usually a clear time frame.
➡️ "I worked at the café last summer."
This means: the job started and ended in the past. It’s over.

"I have been working" is the present perfect continuous tense. It tells us that the action started in the past and is still happening now — or very recently stopped. It connects the past to the present.
➡️ "I have been working at the café for three months."
This means: you started three months ago and are still working there now (or just recently stopped — but the effect is still present).

You can also think of it like this:

  • "I worked" = past done, complete, no link to now
  • "I have been working" = ongoing, or recent, and still relevant now

Imagine two people:

A: "You look tired."
B: "Yeah, I have been working all day."
→ That suggests they just finished or are still working — and that's why they’re tired now.

But if they said:
“Yeah, I worked all day.”
→ That sounds more distant. The work is over, and it might not explain why they’re tired now.

Let me know if you'd like this explained with a story or a more casual example — sometimes that helps cement the difference.

The question

I've got three sad vegetables — a limp carrot, half an onion, some spinach that's seen better days — and one egg. Talk me through dinner.

GPT-4o's answer

Alright, let's turn those sad veggies into something delicious! How about a simple but tasty vegetable stir-fry with a soft scrambled egg on top?

  1. Prep your ingredients:

    • Slice the limp carrot into thin matchsticks or small pieces.
    • Dice the half onion.
    • Roughly chop the spinach.

  2. Cooking the veggies:

    • Heat a bit of oil in a pan over medium heat.
    • Add the onions first, cooking until they become translucent and start to caramelize.
    • Toss in the carrot next, since it needs a bit longer to cook. Sauté for a few minutes until it starts to soften.
    • Add the spinach last, as it will wilt quickly. Stir everything together until the spinach is wilted and everything is well-mixed.

  3. Soft scrambled egg:

    • In a separate non-stick pan, add a bit of butter or oil over low heat.
    • Beat the egg in a bowl, then pour it into the pan.
    • Stir gently and continuously until the egg is just set but still soft and creamy.

  4. Combine:

    • Season the veggies with salt, pepper, and any other seasoning you like (a dash of soy sauce or a sprinkle of chili flakes could be great).
    • Serve the veggies on a plate and top with the soft scrambled egg.

Voilà! You've got a quick, nutritious dinner. What do you think?
